The 1996 Bose ₹2: India’s Withdrawn Centenary Coin

₹2
Denomination: ₹2 Years: 1996 & 1997 Status: Withdrawn (1996) / Corrected (1997) Mints: 4 (1997 issue)

Netaji Subhas Chandra Bose

Netaji Subhas Chandra Bose (23 January 1897 – 18 August 1945) was one of the most prominent and radical leaders of the Indian independence movement. Born in Cuttack, Orissa, he rose to become President of the Indian National Congress twice, in 1938 and 1939, before breaking with the Congress leadership over the question of using armed struggle against the British.

During the Second World War, Bose allied with the Axis powers and led the Azad Hind Fauj — the Indian National Army (INA) — in a military campaign against British India from the east. He famously declared, “Give me blood, and I shall give you freedom.” The title Netaji — meaning “Respected Leader” — was conferred upon him by the INA and remains inseparable from his name.

Bose died in a reported plane crash over Taiwan on 18 August 1945, barely two years before Indian independence, and did not live to see the nation he fought for come into being. His birth centenary — marking 100 years since his birth on 23 January 1897 — fell in the year 1997.

The Gazette Notification and the Error

To mark Bose’s birth centenary, the Ministry of Finance issued a gazette notification on 17 January 1997, authorising the release of a commemorative coin series in four denominations — ₹100, ₹50, ₹10, and ₹2 — all bearing the year 1996. The higher denominations (₹100, ₹50, and ₹10) were designated exclusively for collector sets, while the ₹2 coin was the denomination intended for general circulation.

It was shortly after the initial gazette notification that mint officials recognised a fundamental error: Netaji Subhas Chandra Bose was born on 23 January 1897, making the true centenary of his birth the year 1997, not 1996. Bearing the year 1996 on a coin described as commemorating his birth centenary was incorrect — the coin would have been marking his 99th birth anniversary, not his 100th.

Before the error was caught and corrected, however, the Kolkata Mint had already struck and released a quantity of the ₹2 coin dated 1996 into limited circulation. On 17 February 1997, a fresh gazette notification was issued, correcting the year on all denominations to 1997. This was the first documented instance in the history of Republic India coinage where a corrective action was taken through a fresh notification to supersede an original one, leading to coins with revised details entering circulation.

The 1996-dated ₹2 coin was thereupon withdrawn. The number of coins that had already entered circulation before the recall could be effected remains unknown; an equally unknown quantity was retained or destroyed at the Kolkata Mint. What is certain is that surviving genuine 1996 examples are rare.

Coin Specifications

Both the 1996 and 1997 issues share identical declared specifications. The obverse carries the Ashoka Lion Capital emblem with denomination ₹2, flanked by “भारत” and “INDIA”, with the national motto “सत्यमेव जयते” beneath.

The Withdrawn 1996 Issue

The 1996 coin was struck exclusively at the Kolkata Mint and is the only mint from which the error date coin is known. It carries no mint mark below the year, consistent with Kolkata’s longstanding practice of leaving that position blank on its circulation coins.

Because the coin entered only limited circulation before the withdrawal order took effect, surviving genuine examples — particularly in uncirculated condition — are extremely rare and command a significant premium among collectors.

The Corrected 1997 Issue — All Four Mints

Following the fresh gazette notification of 17 February 1997, the corrected ₹2 coin bearing the year 1997 was struck at all four of India’s active government mints — Kolkata, Mumbai, Hyderabad, and Noida. Collector sets (₹100, ₹50, ₹10, and ₹2) were also reissued with the corrected 1997 date. The 1997 coins are significantly more available than the 1996 issue but are still sought after, particularly from the Hyderabad mint.

Two Ashoka column die varieties are known across the 1997 mints. Type B features an Ashoka column 14 mm tall with 3 furrows on the right lion and 5 lion whiskers. Type C features a column 13 mm tall with 4 furrows on the right lion. Mumbai and Kolkata primarily struck Type C; Hyderabad and Noida are associated with both Types B and C.

Fakes and Doctored Coins

Because genuine 1996 coins are rare and command substantial premiums, a significant number of doctored coins are in circulation. The alteration is straightforward: dishonest dealers file or grind the last digit of the year on a common 1997 coin, converting the “7” into a “6”. The resulting coin can be passed off as the far scarcer 1996 issue to unsuspecting buyers.

Two methods are reliable for distinguishing a genuine 1996 coin from a doctored 1997 coin:

Detection Method 1 — Shape of the Digit “6”

Examine the “6” in the year closely. On a genuine 1996 coin, the digit was struck from a purpose-made die and is well-formed and crisp, with smooth curves and clean edges consistent with the surrounding numerals. On a doctored coin, the “7” has been physically altered by hand — the resulting “6” will typically appear crude, uneven, or clumsily shaped, with tool marks, rough surfaces, or an inconsistency in stroke width compared to the other digits. Any deformation or roughness in the digit alone is cause for suspicion.

Detection Method 2 — Border Thickness (Foolproof)

This is the definitive test. The border (rim) thickness differs measurably between the genuine 1996 and the 1997 coins:

  • Genuine 1996 coin: Thick border — the rim around the coin face is noticeably wide and pronounced.
  • Genuine 1997 coin (and doctored fakes): Thin border — the rim is comparatively narrow.

Examine the border in an area where it is fully visible and undamaged — avoid areas affected by wear or die shift. A doctored 1997 coin will always retain the thin border of its original issue, regardless of how skilfully the digit has been altered. A coin claiming to be a genuine 1996 example with a thin border is a doctored fake.

Where doubt persists — for example, if a coin is worn in a way that obscures the border clearly — both methods should be applied together. Any genuine 1996 coin should satisfy both tests: a correctly formed “6” and a thick border. The combination of a well-formed digit and a convincingly thick border is the strongest assurance of authenticity short of laboratory testing.

Collectors are advised to purchase the 1996 coin only from reputable numismatic dealers with verifiable provenance, and to treat any coin offered at a price significantly below the prevailing market rate with considerable caution.

Collector Notes

The 1996 Bose ₹2 holds a unique place in the catalogue of Republic India commemorative coinage. It is one of the few circulating coins that was officially withdrawn and superseded by a corrected issue, and the only such instance where the error concerned the very year in which a centenary was being marked. The story is documented in the gazette record and is entirely verifiable — making this coin historically significant rather than merely scarce.

Assembling a complete set from this series offers a self-contained numismatic narrative: the withdrawn 1996 error, followed by four mint variants of the corrected 1997 issue (Kolkata, Mumbai, Hyderabad, and Noida). The Hyderabad mint variant is the scarcest among the 1997 issues in uncirculated condition. Collectors seeking the Type B Ashoka column variant have an additional point of distinction to pursue in the Hyderabad and Noida pieces.

Because of the premium commanded by the genuine 1996 coin and the prevalence of doctored examples in the secondary market, due diligence in authentication is essential before any purchase. The thick-border test described above is accessible to any collector without specialist equipment and should be the first check applied to any coin offered as a genuine 1996 example.
